Every single one of your ancestors, without exception, succeeded at mating. Not one of them was left out of the chain. So what exactly did they get right, and how much of it is still running quietly inside you today?

Introduction

Love is universal. Every culture ever studied has it. But so are jealousy, heartbreak, betrayal and divorce. Somewhere between 30 and 50 percent of people have an affair at some point, and every society on earth has some form of divorce. Conflict in mating is not a sign that something broke. It is part of how the system works.

David Buss has spent his career asking why. His answer is that men and women inherited a toolbox of mating strategies, solutions that worked for our ancestors, and that those strategies sometimes collide. To test this, he surveyed more than 10,000 people in 37 cultures across six continents, and later studies in many more countries have backed the findings up.

This summary walks through the whole arc of a relationship, from choosing, to attracting, to keeping, to fighting, to breaking up, and finally to living together in some kind of peace. As you read, remember one rule the author repeats often: evolved does not mean unchangeable, and explaining a behavior is never the same as excusing it.

Lessons you can use

Treat your own mating psychology like weather, not like fate. You can't switch it off, but once you know what it is doing, you can plan for it.

Part 1: Where Our Mating Strategies Come From

Two ways evolution picks winners

Darwin described sexual selection in two forms: competing with members of your own sex, and being chosen by the other sex. The peacock's tail is the classic example. Both forces shaped humans. We compete, and we choose, and so we carry the psychology of both.

Strategies don't need to be conscious

A strategy here is just a solution to a recurring problem: selecting a mate, attracting one, keeping one, and replacing one. The weaverbird builds a nest to attract a female without knowing why. In the same way, you can feel butterflies for someone without once thinking about reproduction. The logic runs in the background, and the feeling is all you notice.

Context changes everything

Culture and circumstance switch strategies on and off. The most powerful switch is the sex ratio. When women outnumber men, commitment gets weaker and casual relationships rise. When men outnumber women, men compete harder to marry and stay. Most people, around 96 to 99 percent, are primarily heterosexual, though female desire in particular tends to be more fluid than male desire.

Lessons you can use

Before you judge a relationship pattern as "just how people are," check the conditions around it: the local sex ratio, the culture, and the person's own circumstances. Most of what looks like fixed human nature is actually a strategy responding to context.

Part 2: What Women Want

Why women are choosier

Women invest more upfront: a large egg, nine months of pregnancy, and years of nursing. A bad choice costs a woman far more than it costs a man, so women evolved to be selective. Birth control has changed the costs, but it has not rewritten the psychology.

The resource preference

Across 37 cultures and studies running from 1939 to 2015, women valued a partner's financial prospects roughly twice as much as men did. Women look for the signals behind resources: ambition, industriousness (the best predictor of future income), social status, intelligence, dependability and emotional stability. Even successful, high-earning women want these qualities as much or more. The "women only want money because they are powerless" theory does not fit the data.

Love, kindness and the deal breakers

Resources alone are never enough. Women everywhere rank kindness, love and commitment at or near the top, and about 89 percent of American women say they would not marry without love. Women also have clear deal breakers. And they match themselves to their own level: you tend to end up with someone close to your own mate value, not far above it.

Lessons you can use

Whether you are the one choosing or the one being chosen, remember the full package: reliability, ambition and kindness matter more over the long run than any single flashy quality. Work on becoming the person you hope to attract.

Part 3: What Men Want

Youth, health and the fertility cues

Men's strongest preferences point to signs of fertility. They prefer partners about two and a half years younger on average, and the gap widens as men age. They respond to clear skin, symmetry, lustrous hair and a waist-to-hip ratio of about 0.70. Even teenage boys prefer slightly older girls, which rules out the idea that men simply want someone they can dominate. Physical appearance matters more to men than to women in every culture studied.

Fidelity and the paternity problem

Because fertilization is hidden inside the woman, ancestral men could never be certain of fatherhood. That uncertainty shaped a lasting priority: sexual faithfulness. The value placed on virginity has dropped in many places, but fidelity still ranks near the top for men.

Why men commit at all

Men commit because women set the terms, because it protects the certainty of paternity, and because children survive better with two parents. Media images of ideal bodies "hijack" these old adaptations and can distort what both sexes believe the other wants. Women, for instance, tend to overestimate how thin men prefer them.

Lessons you can use

Don't trust media images as a picture of real preferences. Knowing that attraction runs on old, automatic cues also helps you see that being "not chosen" by someone says little about your actual worth.

Part 4: Casual Sex

The famous approach experiment

In one Florida study, strangers asked people of the opposite sex for a one-night stand. About 75 percent of the men said yes. Not a single woman did. A French replication found 83 percent of men and 3 percent of women. The gap shows up in fantasies, in the number of partners people say they want (men average about 18 over a lifetime, women four or five), and in how fast desire for a new partner switches on.

Why women do it too

Women have their own reasons for short-term mating: immediate resources, a chance to test a possible long-term partner, a backup in case the current relationship fails, and sometimes the pull of an especially attractive partner. When women do choose short-term partners, they shift their tastes toward physical attractiveness and confident, masculine qualities.

Costs and conditions

The costs are real: disease, damaged reputation, violence and, for women, pregnancy without support. These costs explain why strategies shift with conditions such as father absence, a skewed sex ratio, and how strongly a community disapproves. Regret also differs. Men more often regret the chances they missed. Women more often regret what they did.

Lessons you can use

When expectations clash, don't assume the other person is lying or broken. Talk early and plainly about what each of you wants, because the two sexes often start from different default settings.

Part 5: Attracting a Partner

Men's tactics: resources, commitment and confidence

Buss and his team collected dozens of attraction tactics. Men display resources, from fancy clothes to generosity. Flashy displays work best for short-term interest; ambition and studiousness work best for long-term interest. Showing commitment is one of the strongest moves of all: persistence, kindness, honesty, fidelity, and warmth toward children. Confidence also helps, but genuine confidence beats fake bravado, and women can usually tell the difference.

Women's tactics: appearance and fidelity

Women enhance their looks, using makeup about twenty times as often as men, because looks activate men's evolved preferences. Playing hard to get works best when it is selective: hard for everyone else, available for him. Signaling sexual availability is most effective for short-term attraction, but it can backfire if the goal is a lasting partner.

Putting down the competition

Both sexes belittle rivals. Men mock a rival's resources and ambition. Women mock a rival's looks or reputation. These tactics work only when they hit what the other sex truly values. The most effective one, for either sex, is to suggest the rival would not stay faithful.

Lessons you can use

Honesty and kindness are among the highest-rated attraction signals, and they are the hardest to fake over time. Be consistent, be genuine and be patient. Persistence that respects the other person's wishes tells them you are serious.

Part 6: Staying Together

The threat of mate poachers

Good partners are in short supply, and other people know it. About 60 percent of men and 53 percent of women admit to trying to lure someone else's partner into a long-term relationship, and nearly half of those attempts succeed. A common method is "driving a wedge": praising the target while hinting that their current partner does not appreciate them.

Jealousy as an alarm system

Jealousy evolved as a built-in alarm that warns of a threat to a valuable relationship. Men and women feel it equally strongly, but the triggers differ. Men are more upset by sexual infidelity. Women are more upset by emotional infidelity, because it signals that a partner's time, attention and resources are leaving. The pattern has shown up in surveys, in physiological measurements, in brain scans, and in many different countries.

What actually works for keeping a partner

The best tactic is the simplest: keep giving your partner what drew them to you. For men, that means love, kindness and shared resources. For women, it means looking after their appearance and affection. Darker tactics exist too, such as constant monitoring, controlling a partner's contacts, threats and violence. They sometimes "work" in the narrow sense of preventing defection, but they cause terrible harm, and understanding them is meant to help prevent them, not excuse them.

Lessons you can use

Treat the early qualities as a promise you keep making. If you won the relationship by being kind, attentive and generous, keep doing it. And if jealousy flares, ask what it is protecting before you act on it.

Part 7: Sexual Conflict

Where the two strategies collide

Buss asked hundreds of people what the opposite sex does that upsets them and found 147 distinct complaints. Most of them trace back to a clash of goals. Men often want sex sooner and with fewer strings. Women often want commitment first. Over time, women complain more about neglect and emotional distance, and men complain more about being asked for too much time and attention.

Misreading each other's minds

People constantly guess at hidden intentions, and the guesses are biased in predictable ways. Men tend to over-read sexual interest in friendly behavior, like a smile or a light touch, because the cost of a missed chance was historically higher than the cost of a false alarm. This is called error management. It explains a lot of awkward and sometimes dangerous misunderstandings.

Deception, harassment and violence

Deception is part of the story: men more often exaggerate their commitment, and women more often use sexual cues to get favors. Sexual aggression is rated by women as more upsetting than any of 147 other acts, yet men consistently underestimate how bad it is. Harassment, abuse and sexual assault are real and serious harms. The research is clear that the victims are overwhelmingly women, and that most men do not commit these acts. Scientists still debate whether sexual coercion is a direct adaptation or a by-product of other male traits. Either way, understanding the conditions that trigger it is a path to preventing it.

Lessons you can use

Never assume your reading of someone's interest is correct. Ask, listen, and treat a "no" as final. If you are on the receiving end of pressure or control, trust your discomfort and seek support early.

Part 8: Breaking Up and Changing Over Time

Why relationships end

Divorce exists in every culture. Across 160 societies, the top causes are infidelity and infertility, the two events that most directly damage what a long-term bond was built for. Next come sexual withdrawal, failure to provide, rivalry among co-wives, and cruelty. Because we are always comparing our current partner to the alternatives around us, the mind keeps a quiet running tally of costs and benefits, even in happy relationships.

How people leave and cope

Few people simply walk away. Many try to push the partner into ending it, by pulling back affection, picking fights or creating distance. Breakups rank among the most stressful events in life. Common coping methods are staying friends, talking it over with friends, shopping, new relationships, and sometimes, unhealthily, stalking.

How we change across a lifetime

A woman's value as a mate is tied to fertility and falls with age. A man's value depends more on status and resources and varies much more. In marriage, sexual frequency declines, complaints about affection and attention grow, and men guard younger wives more fiercely. Women's affairs peak toward the end of their fertile years. Men also die earlier than women on average, partly because mate competition pushes them toward risk, which skews the sex ratio against educated women in later life.

Lessons you can use

Long relationships require maintenance. Stay faithful, stay kind, share the load, and keep attending to your partner's sexual and emotional needs. Don't wait for the complaints to double before you act.

Part 9: Harmony Between the Sexes

Similar and different at once

Men and women are the same in most things: both value intelligence, kindness and loyalty above almost everything. They differ mainly where their ancestors faced different problems. Denying those differences does not make them go away, and pretending the sexes are identical does not help anyone. Harmony begins with an honest picture.

Competition inside each sex, not a war between them

Buss argues that it is wrong to see all men as united against women, or the reverse. Men mostly compete with other men, and women mostly compete with other women. Each person shares deep interests with some members of the opposite sex, such as a spouse, a daughter or a mother, and conflicts with others. Women's preferences for status and men's drive to deliver it together helped create men's control of resources, so progress has to address both sides.

We are the first species that can choose

Strategies are flexible, not destiny. Nothing is inevitable: not infidelity, not monogamy, not violence. Fulfilling each other's evolved desires is one of the best predictors of a happy marriage, and a lifelong bond between two unrelated people is one of the great achievements of our species. Modern tools like contraception and dating apps are new, but we meet them with old instincts. The better we understand those instincts, the more freedom we have.

Lessons you can use

Talk openly about what each of you needs and what each of you finds hard. Fulfilling a partner's real desires, instead of the ones you wish they had, is the most reliable route to a peaceful relationship.

Closing: Questions to Reflect On

  • Which of your own relationship expectations are really a reaction to your circumstances, rather than a fixed rule about "how men" or "how women" are?
  • What did your partner (or past partners) first value in you, and are you still giving it?
  • When jealousy shows up, what exactly is it warning you about, and is the alarm accurate?
  • Where might you be misreading someone's signals, or being misread yourself?
  • If you could talk openly about one difference between you and your partner, what would it be?

Every one of us is an evolutionary success story. Now we get to decide what to do with it.
