You walk into the interview room, the big meeting, or onto the stage. You have prepared for weeks. And then your mind goes blank, your voice shrinks, and you hear yourself saying things that don't sound like you at all. It usually isn't a lack of talent or preparation. In that moment, you feel powerless, and powerlessness has a way of separating us from ourselves.

Introduction

This book begins with an ordinary moment. The author, a Harvard social psychologist, is sitting in a bookstore café in Boston when the young woman who served her coffee quietly says thank you. Her name is Fetaine. A teacher had shown her the author's TED talk, and before taking her medical school entrance exam she had stood in the bathroom striking a Wonder Woman pose. It helped. Cuddy says encounters like this have happened again and again, with people of every age and background, all of whom had felt powerless under pressure and found a surprisingly simple way to loosen its grip.

From those stories grows the book's central idea, which Cuddy calls presence: the state of being attuned to our own thoughts, feelings, values, and abilities, and being able to express them comfortably, especially when the stakes are high. It isn't about being flawless, dominant, or charming. It's about showing up as the best version of yourself.

She has a personal reason for caring. In college she was thrown from a car in an accident and suffered a severe head injury. Her IQ dropped by about thirty points, and doctors told her not to expect to finish college. For years she felt like an impostor in her own life. She kept going anyway, and eventually became a professor at Harvard. That experience of feeling small is what made her curious about a simple question: how can we feel more powerful at exactly the moment we need to?

Why does this matter? Because powerlessness is slippery. It can swallow what we know, what we feel, and what we want, and leave us feeling invisible even to ourselves. The woman in the café didn't learn anything new about medicine from a talk. She was helped to let go of a fear that might have blocked what she already knew. That is the promise of presence: not adding ability, but freeing the ability you already have.

The book has eleven chapters. The first ones explain what presence is, why we doubt ourselves, and how power works. The middle ones look at the body. The last ones give practical tools. This summary follows the same order, with one core idea and one practical takeaway for every chapter.

The book in one sentence: presence comes from believing in yourself, and you can train it through your beliefs, the way you see yourself, and a few simple habits of the body.

Part 1: Presence and the Story You Believe

Chapter 1: What Is Presence?

Core idea: presence is alignment. Your words, tone, expression, and body all point in the same direction because you truly believe what you're saying. When they don't, other people sense that something is off, even if they can't say what. They don't think "she's lying." They just feel, "something doesn't add up, and I can't fully trust it."

One of the studies in the book is striking. People who cannot follow spoken words, because of language damage in the brain, turn out to be better at spotting lies. Most of us are so busy listening to words that we miss what the face and body are saying, and the body is much harder to fake. The same goes for us when we perform. If we try to manage every detail, our body falls out of step with our message.

Try this: instead of micromanaging your gestures and phrasing, ask what you truly believe about your topic and about yourself. Prepare the content well, then let your body follow.

Chapter 2: Believing and Owning Your Story

Core idea: authenticity can't be faked, and it can't be memorized either. The chapter draws on a conversation with a celebrated actress, who says that the advice "just do your best" can be paralyzing because it's so vague. What works for her is bringing her whole self into the room. She also accepts that it won't always go well, and that bad feelings pass.

To build that kind of trust in ourselves, the book offers self-affirmation. Not empty slogans like "I'm amazing," but identifying the values, traits, and strengths that matter most to you and affirming them honestly. Research suggests that reminding ourselves of what we value helps us face pressure with more calm.

Two notes matter here. We all have many sides, and in each situation we choose which of our real values to bring forward. That isn't pretending. And affirmations must rest on something true: repeating things you don't believe doesn't help, while affirming what is actually true about you does.

Try this: before an important moment, write down one or two values you hold most deeply and one example of living them. Carry that into the room instead of a list of achievements to show off.

Part 2: Listening and Feeling Like a Fraud

Chapter 3: Stop Preaching, Start Listening

Core idea: presence is contagious. When you are fully there and truly listening, other people feel safe enough to be there too. The book tells of a minister in Boston who helped broker peace among street gangs. On the night a young man was killed, he went to the hospital without the perfect words. He simply gathered people to pray, embraced them, and listened. The lesson is that there isn't always a magic sentence. Sometimes showing up is enough.

The ministers had once planned to preach nonviolence. They realized that would be as ineffective as meeting force with force. So they asked questions instead: what is life on these streets like, and how do you deal with police and rival crews? They listened, and the young men went from being a "problem" to being partners. Cuddy calls this the paradox of listening: by giving up the power to talk and to know best, we become more influential.

The payoffs are concrete. People trust you more. You get real information. You see individuals instead of stereotypes. Solutions built together are more likely to stick. And people who feel heard become more willing to listen back.

Try this: in a difficult conversation, resist the urge to lecture or jump to solutions. Ask sincerely, leave a pause, and listen all the way through. Don't fear a little silence.

Chapter 4: "I Don't Deserve to Be Here"

Core idea: almost everyone has felt like a fraud who is about to be found out. This is impostor syndrome. Cuddy mentions an acclaimed novelist who still imagines the "fraud police" knocking on his door, and a graduation speech in which a musician asked who had ever felt that way, and nearly every hand went up. Oddly, we feel alone in this feeling, and loneliness hurts. The brain even processes social pain in ways that resemble physical pain.

Cuddy is honest that the feeling may never disappear completely. A friend once told a novelist that you never learn how to write a novel, you only learn how to write the one in front of you. The goal is to grow more aware, more willing to talk about it, and better at handling it each time it shows up, like a game of whack-a-mole that you can learn to win.

The logic is worth noticing. If nearly everyone feels like a fraud, then either everyone is a fraud, or our judgment of ourselves is off. The second is far more likely. We stay quiet out of shame, and the silence makes the feeling seem more personal and more heavy. That's why the simplest step, talking, is often the most powerful.

Try this: tell one person you trust about a time you felt like an impostor.

Part 3: Power and Body Language

Chapter 5: How Powerlessness Shackles the Self

Core idea: the feeling of power changes how we think, feel, and act. People who feel powerful focus more on their goals, act more boldly, and worry less about what others think. Feeling powerless does the opposite: we shrink, grow anxious, and find it hard to think clearly. This is about the feeling of power, not a job title, and that feeling can be influenced.

Cuddy separates two kinds of power. Social power is control over other people, power over. It's limited. Personal power is freedom from other people's domination, the power to be yourself. It's unlimited, and it is what opens the door to presence. Someone can hold a high position and still feel powerless, and the reverse is true as well.

There's a flip side. Research from the author's colleagues shows that powerful people tend to pay less attention to people below them. Attention flows upward, not downward. The good news is that when people are asked to feel responsible for others, they become more attentive. Healthy power means having courage while still seeing other people.

Try this: before a stressful situation, briefly write about a time you felt in control. And when you hold influence, ask yourself: who am I not paying attention to?

Chapter 6: Slouching, Steepling, and the Language of the Body

Core idea: our bodies speak about power, and others read them fast. Expansive, open postures signal power. Small, closed postures signal the opposite. Studies of athletes from many countries, including those who were blind from birth, show the same expression of pride after a win: chest out, shoulders back, arms raised. That suggests the display is built in, not learned by imitation.

The chapter also discusses gestures like steepling, pressing the fingertips together in a triangle, which signals confidence. And it points to a modern habit that shrinks us: hunching over our phones.

There's a caution too. Dominant postures can be contagious in an unconscious way, and overdoing them in front of others tends to make them shrink rather than admire us. That's why the book later separates big private poses from subtle, open posture during interactions.

Try this: notice when your body shrinks: shoulders forward, arms tight, head down. Become aware of the pattern, then open your posture gradually.

Reflection questions
  • In which situations do you shrink without noticing?
  • Who do you tend to give more attention to, and who tends to go unnoticed?

Part 4: How the Body Shapes the Mind

Chapter 7: Surfing, Smiling, and Singing

Core idea: mind and body are one system. We tend to think the brain simply gives orders to the body, but the traffic runs both ways. The book points to several findings. People who hold a pen between their teeth, which engages the smile muscles, rate cartoons as funnier. Researchers have even tried using Botox to relax the frown muscles as an add-on treatment for depression. And an old idea says we don't sing because we're happy, we're happy because we sing.

The Botox findings deserve a careful look. In one randomized trial with patients whose depression had resisted treatment, those injected in the forehead saw their depression scores fall by about half in six weeks, compared with roughly ten percent for the placebo group. The book admits some early studies are hard to interpret; the simple lesson is that expressions help shape what we feel.

The central metaphor is surfing. Cuddy describes learning to surf and realizing that progress came not from mastering every technique, but from deciding to stay on the board. That decision built confidence with every attempt.

Try this: use the body as a lever. Before a tense situation, take a slow, deep breath, allow a small smile, or hum a tune. And decide to stay on the board, which means staying with it even when you still feel clumsy.

Chapter 8: The Body Shapes the Mind, So "Starfish Up!"

Core idea: an expansive posture can help us feel more powerful. Cuddy recalls how, after her accident, she would curl up like a pill bug in the passenger seat, which only made her feel more anxious. That planted a question: what if the body opened up instead?

The research behind her talk began with a worry in the classroom. At her business school, participation made up half the grade, yet many bright students shrank and stayed silent. The team designed an experiment. Participants didn't know the real purpose, held two brief poses totaling about two minutes, then reported how they felt and took part in a small gambling task. According to the book, those who held expansive poses felt more powerful and were more willing to take risks, and early results also showed hormonal changes. We'll look at that last claim in the critical note below. Kids in the book even learn to "starfish up," stretching out like a sea star when nervous.

Try this: before a hard moment, stand or sit in an open posture for two minutes, in private. Think of it as a warm-up, not a guarantee.

Part 5: How to Pose and How to Nudge Yourself

Chapter 9: How to Pose for Presence

Core idea: there are two stages. Before the important moment, use a big pose while you're alone. During the moment itself, skip the big pose, because it can break social norms and make other people feel threatened. Switch to an upright, open posture that is subtle.

WhenWhat to do
Before the momentA big pose in private: a bedroom, an elevator, a bathroom stall. If you must sit, stretch an arm over the back of the chair. If you can't move, imagine yourself in the widest pose possible.
During the momentShoulders back, chest open, slow deep breaths, chin level, feet planted. Use open gestures, avoid pinning your arms to your sides like penguin wings, and allow yourself pauses.
Through the daySet posture reminders, arrange your desk so you have to stretch, and stand or walk now and then.

When might you pose? Before job interviews, negotiations, auditions, presentations, hard conversations, or asking for help. Arrive early so you feel at home in the space, and skip the hunched-over phone check in the waiting room.

A five-minute routine before an important moment

(This routine is the summarizer's own, combining the book's suggestions. Adjust it to fit you.)

  1. Minute one: find a private spot and take a few slow, deep breaths.
  2. Minutes two and three: stand open, shoulders back, chest open. Feel your feet on the floor.
  3. Minute four: recall one value you hold dearly and one time you felt powerful.
  4. Minute five: say one honest sentence about what you want to convey, then walk in with an upright, open posture, not a big pose.
Chapter 10: Self-Nudging

Core idea: we can "nudge" ourselves in a better direction through small changes in our environment and habits. Take saving money. When thousands of people were asked who their worst enemy was, about half answered: themselves. We often treat our future self like a stranger. In experiments, people who saw an age-progressed photo of themselves saved about twice as much.

Clothing matters too. In one study, people wearing a white lab coat paid more attention, especially when told it was a doctor's coat. What we wear influences how we think and act.

Try this: write a letter to yourself ten years from now before making a big decision, put reminders where you'll see them, and wear clothes that make you feel ready.

Reflection questions
  • What small change to your desk, phone, or morning could help you show up more fully?
  • What would you, ten years from now, want to say to the person you are today?

Part 6: Fake It Until You Become It

Chapter 11: Fake It Till You Become It

Core idea: this phrase is often misunderstood. Cuddy isn't telling us to deceive others. She's suggesting we "trick" ourselves a little by acting as if we're already more powerful, until the feeling grows real, and then keep practicing, even if it takes time. She quotes a classical thinker: begin now to be what you will be hereafter.

The book closes with the story of a young woman whose boss treated her inappropriately. After sharing the situation with friends, she decided to confront him. That morning she stood tall alone for a few minutes, then walked in and spoke calmly. She felt strong without needing to dominate, strong enough to be compassionate. He ended up apologizing.

Why small steps? Because moments accumulate. Each time you show up, your mind, feelings, and body learn that it's possible. Moving, even awkwardly, beats standing still.

Try this: choose one small moment this week that you'd normally avoid, and face it with one small step. No need for perfection. Just a bit braver than yesterday, then repeat.

Three honest ways to apply it

(These suggestions are the summarizer's own, in the spirit of the chapter, not from the book.)

  • Start small and real. Pick an action that still feels honest to you, like asking one question in a meeting rather than leading the whole thing.
  • Don't play a character who isn't you. The aim is to move toward your best self, not to become someone else.
  • Celebrate small progress. Note every moment you showed up more fully than before. That's the evidence that builds your confidence.

A Critical Note on the Power-Posing Research

Before closing, there's something readers today should know. The part about power poses, specifically hormones and risk-taking, has since been disputed.

  • Replications didn't fully succeed. Other researchers who repeated the study found that expansive poses raised people's feelings of power, but did not change hormones or willingness to take risks.
  • A co-author stepped back. In 2016, one of the lead researchers on the original study said she no longer believed the power-pose effects were real and acknowledged weaknesses in the original methods.
  • The author defends a narrower claim. Cuddy says she stands by the effect on feelings of power, but has not taken a firm position on hormones.
  • Later reviews. Some combined analyses find a fairly consistent effect on self-reported feelings and mood, but weak evidence for real changes in hormones or behavior.
ClaimCurrent status
Expansive posture increases feelings of powerFairly consistent in self-reports, though some researchers consider the evidence limited
Expansive posture changes testosterone and cortisolNot confirmed in replications
Expansive posture increases willingness to take risksNot confirmed in replications
Open posture helps you feel calmer and more composedPlausible as a personal habit, though real-world impact varies

A healthy way to read it: treat an open posture as a small, cheap way to feel more ready, not as a hormone hack and not as a guarantee of success. Some other findings in the book, such as the effect of smiling, are also still debated. And the readers' stories in the book are moving, but they aren't scientific proof.

Summary and Closing

Ten things to remember
  1. Presence is alignment. Words, tone, and body agree because you believe in yourself.
  2. Authenticity can't be faked. Bring yourself, not a role.
  3. Affirming your values helps. Remember what matters most to you.
  4. Presence is contagious. Being there and listening helps others be there too.
  5. Almost everyone feels like a fraud. Talk about it instead of carrying it alone.
  6. Feeling powerful changes how you think. Keep noticing other people when you have power.
  7. The body speaks. Notice when you shrink.
  8. The body influences feelings. Breath, smile, and posture are small levers.
  9. Preparing and performing differ. Big poses before, subtle posture during.
  10. Small steps, repeated. Shape your environment, write to your future self, and keep practicing.

Closing

Presence isn't a trait reserved for a handful of charismatic people. It's something we practice, moment by moment. You may never feel completely free of nerves or doubt. That's normal. What can change is what you do in the middle of those feelings.

One more thing is easy to forget: presence doesn't mean never failing. There will be days when you prepare, stand tall, and still stumble. Like the actress in the second chapter, you can let the bad feeling come, because it will pass. What matters is that you come back for the next moment.

So today, pick one small moment. Square your shoulders, take a breath, remember one value you hold, and walk in. You don't have to be perfect. You only have to be there as yourself.
