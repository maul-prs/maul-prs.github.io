You can work hard, earn more every year, and still not get any richer. I've watched it happen to smart, decent people. The problem isn't the money. It's what's going on between their ears, and the good news is that it can be trained.

Why Money Alone Doesn't Make You Rich

Gold taught me an expensive lesson

In 1972, when I was twenty-five, I started buying gold coins at around $70 an ounce. By 1980 gold was nearing $800, and the rumor mill said $2,500 was on the way. People who'd never bought gold in their lives piled in. I got greedy too. Instead of selling some and taking a small profit, I held on, hoping for more. A year later gold fell below $500 and I sold my last coin. It kept sliding until it bottomed at $250 in 1999.

That cost me, but it taught me that if you can lose money investing in real money, you can lose money in anything. It isn't gold, stocks, real estate, or hard work that makes you rich. It's what you know about them.

Golf lessons or golf clubs

A friend of mine spends thousands a year on the latest clubs and gadgets and won't spend a dime on lessons. His game never improves. Billions of people do the same thing with money. They pour their hard-earned cash into stocks and real estate and almost nothing into information, and so their financial score stays about the same.

The rules changed

The advice you hear everywhere is: work hard, save money, get out of debt, live below your means, and invest in a well-diversified portfolio of mutual funds. I call it obsolete. The rules of money changed in 1971, when the dollar stopped being money and became a currency. Saving a currency that keeps losing value makes you a loser under the new capitalism. Here's the equation I use: information plus education equals knowledge. Without financial education, people can't turn the flood of information into something useful.

This book isn't financial advice, and it's not a magic formula. It's about getting richer by getting smarter.

TakeawayStop asking "what should I buy?" and start asking "what do I know about this?" Put some of your money into learning before you put it into assets.

What Financial Intelligence Really Is

Two dads, two kinds of money problems

My poor dad, a brilliant educator, had money problems all his life. No matter what he earned, the problem was not enough. My rich dad, who wasn't nearly as academically smart, had the opposite problem: too much money. I learned early that rich or poor, we all have money problems. The question is which kind you want.

Money by itself doesn't fix money problems. Look at MC Hammer, lottery winners, and pro athletes who go broke. Hard work, degrees, and jobs don't fix them either. What fixes them is financial intelligence, the part of our overall intelligence we use to solve financial problems.

A money problem is like a toothache

Rich dad said a money problem is like a toothache. Ignore it and you feel bad, you do poorly at work, you lose your job, you can't pay rent, and eventually you're on the street, still with the toothache. In real life, it's using credit cards to cover shortfalls, then a home equity loan to pay off the cards, then new cards to pay off the old ones. Nobody pulls the weed up by the root, which is the spending habit. Every problem you solve makes you smarter and ready for a bigger one.

Three ways people respond

The poor see money problems only as problems and feel like victims. The middle class try to outsmart them with schooling, secure jobs, a house, and a retirement plan, and they often wake up at fifty as prisoners in their own office. The rich welcome financial problems, because they know problems are how you get smarter. Rich dad used golf as a metaphor: money is my score, and my financial statement is my scorecard.

Two rule changes made all this more urgent. In 1971 the dollar became a currency, which has to keep moving or lose value. In 1974 companies began shifting from pensions that paid for life to 401(k)s that left workers on their own, even though nobody taught them how to invest.

TakeawayWrite down your biggest money problem today and pick its root cause to solve first. Each problem you solve raises your financial IQ.

The Five Financial IQs

Intelligence versus IQ

Financial intelligence is what we use to solve money problems. Financial IQ is how we measure it. If two people each earn $100,000 and one pays 20 percent in taxes while the other pays 50 percent, the one who keeps $80,000 has a higher financial IQ than the one who keeps $50,000.

The five

  • IQ #1, making more money, measured in gross dollars.
  • IQ #2, protecting your money, measured by the percentage you lose to taxes and predators.
  • IQ #3, budgeting your money, measured by the surplus you create. Someone earning $30,000 who lives on $25,000 and invests $5,000 beats someone earning $70,000 who spends it all.
  • IQ #4, leveraging your money, measured by return on investment, especially after taxes.
  • IQ #5, improving your financial information, the foundation for the other four.

Who needs it most

I don't think financial intelligence is the most important intelligence. Academic, professional, and health intelligence matter a great deal, and my doctor is proof. But money affects everything that's important. Using the CASHFLOW Quadrant, employees (E) and self-employed or specialists (S) can get by on less financial intelligence. For business owners (B) and investors (I), it's everything, because it's what gets you paid. As rich dad said, you can be a successful doctor or schoolteacher and be poor, but you can't be a successful entrepreneur or investor and be poor.

I also want to be straight with you: I can't guarantee anything. I only write about what I do or have done. As rich dad put it, answers are about the past, and abilities are about the future.

TakeawayScore yourself on all five IQs and pick the weakest one. Work on that one first, since the five are tied together.

IQ #1: Making More Money

Choosing learning over a paycheck

After the Merchant Marine Academy I sailed as a third mate for Standard Oil, then left that good pay to join the Marines and fly in Vietnam. When I got out, I could have gone back to the oil company or flown for the airlines. Instead I took a job at Xerox in Honolulu for $720 a month. I wasn't after the pay. I was shy, terrified of rejection, and couldn't sell, and Xerox offered sales training. I nearly got fired in the first two years, then became number one in the branch.

Wallets, pirates, and joining them

My nylon and Velcro surfer wallet business took off, then collapsed when cheap copies flooded the market. I saved it by licensing rock band names. Then pirates started copying my licensed products. After a few months I realized the only people getting rich were my attorneys, so I fired them, flew to Asia, and licensed the pirates to make my wallets. My costs dropped, quality improved, and I went back to doing what I do best: selling.

The process makes you rich

Every worthwhile goal has a process, and the process, not the money, makes you rich. That's why lottery winners and inheritors so often end up broke. Emotional intelligence matters here. Warren Buffett says, "If you cannot control your emotions, you cannot control your money." Rich dad told me, "You can quit when you win, but never quit because you're losing." Choose your challenges deliberately, and look at life as a learning adventure.

Work for assets, not just income

E's and S's work for money. B's and I's work for assets that produce cash flow or appreciation. Earned income is the hardest income to protect, while passive income is taxed the least. Many self-employed people own a job, not a business, because if they stop working, the income stops. Each year Kim and I set goals for how many new assets we want, not how much money. Kim now has more than a thousand rental units.

The broadest way to make money is to solve other people's problems. I also have little patience for people who want to be paid more to do less, and I've said as much about unions. That's my opinion, and plenty of people disagree.

TakeawayChoose work for what it will teach you, not just what it pays, and move step by step from selling your time to owning assets.

IQ #2: Protecting Your Money

Measured in percentages

Take three people earning $100,000. One earns it as wages and may pay as much as 50 percent in combined taxes. One earns it from investments and pays 15 percent. The third pays nothing. The lower the percentage predators take, the higher the IQ #2. Rich dad taught my friend and me with "bunnies, birds, and bugs": some of the biggest thieves of your wealth are people and institutions you love and trust. Then he gave us the seven B's.

The seven B's

  • Bureaucrats: taxes keep growing because they throw money at problems rather than solving them. Wages get the least protection.
  • Bankers: hidden fees in 401(k)s and mutual funds, and a system that erodes the value of savings.
  • Brokers: many are salespeople, not investors. Beware churning and round-trip costs, and look for brokers who teach and invest in what they sell.
  • Businesses: store credit cards and products that make you poorer.
  • Brides and beaus: divorce without a prenuptial agreement can cost half your wealth. Plan your exit before you enter.
  • Brothers-in-law: death brings family and taxes out of the woodwork. Wills and trusts decide who gets what.
  • Barristers: lawsuits. Keep nothing of value in your own name, buy liability insurance before you need it, and hold assets in legal entities such as corporations or LLCs.

The rules changed again

I point out that in 1943 the government began taking taxes out of paychecks before workers got paid, and in 1974 workers had to invest on their own through 401(k)s, whose withdrawals are taxed as earned income. I also write about the Federal Reserve as a bank owned by rich people and cite R. Buckminster Fuller's book Grunch of Giants. Those are my views and my reading, and you should check them yourself. Always get advice from qualified tax attorneys and accountants.

I'm not trying to change the system. It's easier to change myself. I just want to know the rules and play by them.

TakeawayFigure out what percentage of your income goes to taxes, fees, and legal exposure. Then shift toward better-protected income and structures, with professional help.

IQ #3: Budgeting Your Money

Deficit or surplus

My poor dad said, "Live below your means." My rich dad said, "Expand your means." A budget is a plan, and there are only two kinds: deficit, where you spend more than you earn, and surplus, where income exceeds spending. Rich dad loved the words "excess of income." A surplus comes less from cutting back than from earning more, which brings us back to IQ #1. A business with a deficit needs to increase sales, not just trim costs.

Four budget tips

Tip 1: a surplus is an expense. Soon after Kim and I married, we told our bookkeeper Betty to take 30 percent of all income off the top and put it into the asset column, for saving, tithing, and investing. Then she paid taxes, liabilities, and bills with the rest. We came up short by as much as $4,000 some months. That shortfall forced us back to IQ #1, and we hustled for extra income. The pressure from creditors drove us to make more money. If 30 percent is too much, start at 3 percent. Today we send about 80 percent of our income to the asset column.

Tip 2: the expense column is a crystal ball. Look at what a person spends discretionary money on, such as investment books and seminars versus six-packs and potato chips, and you can predict the future.

Tip 3: my assets pay for my liabilities. My poor dad said, "We can't afford it." My rich dad asked, "How can I afford it?" I wanted a $200,000 Bentley. Instead of paying cash and watching it lose value, I had my broker turn $200,000 into $450,000, then bought the car from the proceeds and kept my original $200,000.

Tip 4: spend to get rich. When sales drop, companies cut advertising and make things worse. When Kim and I were in trouble, we poured effort into sales and marketing.

Good debt and bad debt

Good debt makes you richer and is paid by someone else, such as tenants. Bad debt makes you poorer and you pay it yourself. When one of my businesses failed and left me owing about $400,000, Kim and I kept tithing, saving, and investing while we paid every dollar back.

TakeawayPay yourself first by moving a fixed percentage of every income into assets before paying anything else. Then cover the gap by earning more, not by deprivation.

IQ #4: Leveraging Your Money

The day the market fell

On August 9, 2007, the market plunged and the Fed pumped in cash. A financial planner on TV said to stay the course, invest for the long term in mutual funds, and avoid real estate. That same day Kim and I closed on a $17 million, 300-unit apartment house in Tulsa. The difference came down to two words: control and leverage. Most people hold paper assets (savings, stocks, bonds, mutual funds) that they can't control.

Leverage works only with control

Leverage means doing more with less. The bank financed 80 percent of the apartments, a 1-to-4 ratio. Using leverage on something you don't control is like buying a car without a steering wheel and stomping on the gas. I can control all four columns of the financial statement:

  • Income: raise rents about $100 a unit through improvements, washers and dryers, and market adjustments, which adds $360,000 a year, financed by the bank.
  • Expenses: cut administrative costs, turnover, and operating costs through strong property management.
  • Liabilities: low interest rates, about 5.5 percent blended.
  • Assets: value grows from operations, not from the market.

I also explain why I think net worth is worthless. It's often an opinion, it's often based on possessions that lose value, and it can rise simply because the dollar is falling.

Seven points and three advanced strategies

There are many types of leverage. Paper assets give little control. Higher returns don't have to mean higher risk when you have control and education. Most financial advisors are salespeople. Diversification is, as Buffett says, protection against ignorance. Professionals focus and hedge, for example with insurance.

I separate investing for capital gains from investing for cash flow, and describe three advanced strategies. OPM, other people's money. ROI, where I count only the cash that actually lands in my pocket. And IRR, which captures depreciation, amortization paid by tenants, and appreciation. I call a return with none of my own money invested "infinite." At exit, I refinance instead of selling, pulling out money tax-free while keeping the asset.

Understand the limits, though. Leverage is safe only when control and skill are real. I tell people to start small. Kim's first property cost $45,000, with $5,000 down and $25 a month in cash flow.

TakeawayUse leverage only on assets you can control and understand, start small, and measure your results by the cash that actually reaches your pocket.

IQ #5: Improving Your Financial Information

Lessons from the top-secret room

In Vietnam I worked as an assistant to my squadron's top-secret information officer, turning raw data into information for the commanders. That's where I learned that information can mean life or death in war and rich or poor in business. I describe four ages: hunter-gatherer, agrarian, industrial, and information. In the Information Age, information leveraged by technology is wealth. Obsolete information makes people poor, and overload makes them freeze.

How I sort information

I classify information by time (it goes stale fast), credibility (most people get financial information from other people who are short of money), classification (the closer to the inside, the better, and the average investor always arrives late), relationship to other information (reading trends), and deception, such as pump-and-dump schemes or sleight-of-hand labels like "low fat" on a sugary cereal.

Then come six lessons:

  • Facts versus opinions. Price predictions and home appraisals are opinions. An insane solution is treating an opinion as a fact.
  • Risky actions come from investing on opinions.
  • Control over the asset, like setting my own rents.
  • The rules. Without rules, assets lose value.
  • Trends. The trend is your friend.

The woman with red teeth

In 1972 and 1973 I noticed South Vietnamese civilians trading their currency for gold. A friend and I flew north, just behind enemy lines, hoping to buy gold below the world price of about $82. An old woman chewing betel nuts wouldn't budge. She knew the global price, and she knew desperate buyers would pay it. I learned three things: gold has one global price, follow the trend rather than hunting a discount, and information only matters when intelligence gives it meaning.

Trends I was following when I wrote this: oil, silver (which I thought undervalued, with gold-to-silver at about 50 to 1), and rental housing. I also watch the roughly twenty-year cycle between stocks and commodities that Jim Rogers describes, and I treat a skyline full of construction cranes as a sign a boom is ending. I could be wrong, so do your own research.

TakeawayBefore you act, ask: is this a fact or an opinion, who is it from, and how close are they to the source? Watch the trend, not the daily bounce.

The Integrity of Money

All five intelligences must work together

I use "integrity" to mean wholeness. A car stops if one system fails, and a body gets sick if one system clogs. Wealth works the same way. If one of the five financial intelligences is missing, the symptoms show up as low income, heavy taxes, excessive debt, and sometimes bankruptcy. I describe a friend who earns a lot but has no tax protection, spends impulsively, and depends on her home as her leverage. They're good people with no personal financial statement.

A financial statement is your financial report card. As rich dad said, my banker has never asked me for my report card.

Warren Buffett's intrinsic value

Buffett doesn't diversify. He looks for businesses with intrinsic value. I boil it down to four traits: a protected niche (like Coca-Cola's trademark), leverage (the business doesn't depend on the owner), expandability (it can grow across the world), and predictability (the money arrives rain or shine). I apply this to my own brand. The Rich Dad name is trademarked, my books are licensed to publishers in 109 countries, and I turned down a mutual fund endorsement that would have been false to the brand. As Buffett says, accounting is the language of business.

Government, storms, and preparation

I believe the U.S. government lost financial integrity in 1971, and that rising debt and taxes are the symptoms. That's my political opinion, not settled fact. I expect storms ahead and offer three recommendations:

  • Put your house in order. Pick the weakest of your five intelligences and fix that one first.
  • Invest in assets with intrinsic value. Practice by analyzing businesses and properties around you.
  • Batten down the hatches. A ship's crew that has trained for a typhoon is the one that comes out the other side.
TakeawayBuild your personal financial statement now, then pick the single weakest intelligence and repair it before the next storm arrives.

Developing Your Financial Genius

The average student who chose differently

I never felt smart in school. We lived on the "poor" side of the river, but I attended the school for rich kids, and I crossed a bridge every time I visited my friends' homes. By the time I left the Marines I knew I didn't want to be average. "Live below your means and diversify" is average advice for average results. In every other field people are pushed to be the best. With money they're told to spread out and cut back. When Wall Street's quant funds all lost money in 2007, it was because the smartest graduates all used the same models and sold at the same time.

Seven intelligences, three brains

Howard Gardner's work showed me seven intelligences: linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, spatial, interpersonal, and intrapersonal. Schools reward mostly the first two. Mine are spatial, kinesthetic, and intrapersonal. I also describe three brains: the left (logic and language), the right (creativity), and the subconscious, the most powerful, which fights, flees, or freezes. You can know exactly what to do with your left and right brains and still be stopped by a subconscious that whispers, "That's too risky." Intrapersonal intelligence is the ability to choose your subconscious response.

Environment is the strongest teacher

Mirror neurons mean our brains copy what we see. The poor, middle class, and rich really do speak different dialects about money. That's why changing your environment can change your life. I chose to be an entrepreneur and real estate investor so I could surround myself with smart people on a team. A 2005 Arizona State University study backed my CASHFLOW game as the second-best way to learn, just below real life, according to the Cone of Learning, because games engage all three brains. To be fair, those results come with the usual caveats, and I'm not claiming any guarantees.

TakeawayPick environments that train all three brains, and notice when your subconscious, not your logic, is doing the talking.

Training Your Financial IQ Through Environments and Feedback

Transitional environments

Instead of handing your money to an advisor for the "long term," I suggest transitional environments that strengthen all three brains:

  • School: trains the left and right brain but often feeds the fear of failing.
  • Church: spiritual strength steadies the subconscious.
  • The military: builds courage and self-control.
  • Network marketing: sales training and a supportive environment.
  • Business: a small company teaches every part of a business, a big one trains your nerves.
  • Seminars: hands-on practice for would-be entrepreneurs and investors.
  • Coaching: a coach helps you move from point A to point B.
  • CASHFLOW Clubs: a community that plays and learns together.

Some of these are products from my own company, so judge for yourself which ones fit you.

Ignorance and courage

A friend of mine says entrepreneurs have two characteristics: ignorance and courage. I know a gifted hairstylist who talks about opening his own salon but still rents a single chair. A former flight attendant with no experience, but plenty of courage, just opened a salon of her own. Courage is worth more than good grades in the real world.

Free feedback

The world is constantly giving you feedback: the taxes on your pay stub, a creditor's phone call, working harder and still not earning enough, a home that makes you feel poor. There are three things to know. Have the courage to be open to feedback. Offer feedback only when it's asked for. And be wary of con men who tell you what you want to hear. I close by asking: are you living where you want to live, at the standard you want, with the people you want around you?

TakeawayJoin one learning environment and one mentor or community in the next 30 days, then watch the feedback your spending, taxes, and results are already giving you.
