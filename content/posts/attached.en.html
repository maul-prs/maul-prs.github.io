Our friend Tamara fell for Greg. He said things like “You don’t have to be home all by yourself, you can come work over at my place,” and “You can call me any time you like.” It felt like a promise of never being alone. But Greg had also mentioned that he had never had a stable relationship, that he always grew tired of girlfriends and moved on. Tamara heard the promise, not the warning. She believed what many of us grow up believing: that love conquers all. She was wrong.

Introduction: The New Science of Adult Attachment

Chapter 1: Decoding Relationship Behavior

Psychiatrist Amir Levine and psychologist Rachel Heller start with three problems that look unrelated: an anxious woman who obsesses over whether he’ll call, a successful man who gets bored and feels trapped after a few weeks, and a wife who feels alone in her marriage because her husband won’t talk. As clinicians they were taught that every case is unique and must be traced through each person’s history. Then they found one explanation for all of them: attachment theory, pioneered by John Bowlby and later developed into research on adult love.

By this theory, people fall into three main styles: roughly half are secure, about 20 percent anxious, and about 25 percent avoidant. The remaining 3 to 5 percent fall into a fourth, less common disorganized category.

SecureComfortable with closeness and independent.
AnxiousCraves closeness, alert to signs of rejection.
AvoidantKeeps distance, treats independence as essential.
Takeaway: Many relationship problems aren’t about too little love but about different needs for closeness.

Chapter 2: Dependency Is Not a Bad Word

Our need to connect with someone special is part of our biology and has nothing to do with how self-sufficient we are. Once we choose a partner, dependency always exists. Evolution made a couple into one physiological unit: if he is upset, I am unsettled too. That gives rise to the dependency paradox: the better we can depend on a supportive partner, the safer we feel being independent and adventurous. A partner who acts as a secure base lets us take risks.

All styles share the need

Secure and anxious types embrace this pull, while avoidants suppress it. But research shows avoidants still have the need. They just work to hold it down.

Takeaway: Needing a partner is not weakness. Look for someone who can be your secure base, and be that for them.

Part One: Your Relationship Toolkit

Chapter 3: Step One, What Is My Attachment Style?

Attachment styles are stable but plastic.Levine and Heller, Chapter 3

The first step is knowing yourself. The book offers a questionnaire adapted from the ECR (published in 1998 by Brennan, Clark, and Shaver), which measures two dimensions: anxiety in the relationship and avoidance. Styles are fairly stable but plastic: they can shift through experience, including time with a secure person.

Takeaway: Learn your style without judging yourself. It is a map, not a verdict.

Chapter 4: Step Two, What Is My Partner’s Style?

After you understand yourself, work out your partner’s or prospective partner’s style from behavioral clues. One step: assess how they react when you communicate effectively, that is, state your needs honestly and directly. A secure person tends to respond with care, while someone who shuts down or shrinks your concern is worth noting.

Takeaway: Watch actions and reactions, not just the sweet talk in the beginning. A promise of closeness doesn’t always match the ability to be close.

Part Two: The Three Styles in Everyday Life

Chapter 5: Living with a Sixth Sense for Danger, the Anxious Style

Anxious people have a “sixth sense” for signs of threat in a relationship, and their attachment system tends to stay switched on. They use protest behavior: anything that jolts the other person into noticing and responding. In an fMRI study, anxious women had a harder time shutting off thoughts about relationship scenarios. Protest behavior often damages the very relationship they are trying to save.

Takeaway: When you feel like calling again and again or testing your partner, ask: what do I really need? Then say it directly.

Chapter 6: Keeping Love at Arm’s Length, the Avoidant Style

Avoidant people need closeness but suppress it with deactivating strategies, any behavior or thought that squelches intimacy. The more you use them, the lonelier and less happy you become.

Chapter 7: Getting Comfortably Close, the Secure Style

Secure people are comfortable with closeness and stay independent. They state their needs clearly, don’t play push and pull, and don’t treat intimacy as a threat. A secure partner can be a secure base, and over time anxious and avoidant people next to them often become calmer.

Takeaway: A consistent, responsive partner is the best investment. And be that person for your partner.

Part Three: When Attachment Styles Clash

Chapter 8: The Anxious-Avoidant Trap

Janet and Mark, together eight years, spent two years arguing about buying a washing machine. Mark wanted one, Janet didn’t. But when Janet did laundry, she went to her sister’s place and idled the day away. Janet is avoidant, always finding things to do without Mark. For Mark, who is anxious, the machine really stood for something else: being close to Janet. The washing machine fight was only a symptom of different needs for closeness.

Anxious partner moves closer→Avoidant partner pulls away→Anxiety grows
Takeaway: When you fight over something small, ask: what need for closeness is hiding behind this?

Chapter 9: Escaping the Anxious-Avoidant Trap

Anxious-avoidant couples can find greater security. The book provides a relationship inventory: write down the triggering situation, your reaction, the thoughts that arise, your feelings, and what you lose by it, and then identify a more secure role. Spot the distorted thoughts, like mind reading (“I know he’s leaving me”), all-or-nothing thinking, and “I knew this was too good to last.”

Takeaway: Map your pattern when you’re calm. Knowing your triggers lets you choose a more secure response.

Chapter 10: When Abnormal Becomes the Norm

In some cases even repeated efforts fail, and the clash of these styles can become truly harmful. The book gives examples of mistreatment that gets normalized: snapped at for gazing lovingly, mocked for walking slowly, demeaned in intimacy. The idea that only “pathetic” people put up with this is wrong. Anxious and avoidant people can bring out the worst in each other, until “abnormal” becomes the norm.

When to leave

If a relationship keeps demeaning you and change doesn’t come, ending it may be the healthiest choice.

Takeaway: Love doesn’t require you to stay in demeaning treatment.

Part Four: The Secure Way, Sharpening Your Skills

Chapter 11: Effective Communication

Effective communication means getting your needs across clearly without attacking. Its five principles:

1. Wear your heart on your sleeveBe fully honest about feelings. Be emotionally brave.
2. Focus on your needsUse verbs like need, feel, want.
3. Be specificSay exactly what bothers you.
4. Don’t blameTalk when calm, not about to explode.
5. Be assertive, not apologeticYour needs are valid.
Example from the book

“I feel devalued when you contradict me in front of your friends. I need to feel that you respect my opinions.”

Takeaway: Swap “you always…” for “when you…, I feel… and I need….”

Chapter 12: Working Things Out, Five Secure Principles

Healthy couples don’t treat conflict as a zero-sum game. Frank loves the family summer home in the Berkshires, and Sandy hates it. After a few bitter fights they agreed to take turns: sometimes the woods, sometimes the city, with Frank scheduling outdoor activities so he stays sane. The five secure principles for conflict:

Care about their well-being→Stay on the problem→Don’t generalize→Be willing to engage→Communicate feelings and needs
Takeaway: Your partner’s well-being matters as much as your own. Ignoring it comes back to hurt you.

Three Questions to Sit With

  1. When I feel insecure in a relationship, which protest behavior or deactivating strategy do I use most?
  2. Do I judge a prospective partner by their promises, or by their actions over several months?
  3. Which needs have I kept to myself because I thought I was “asking too much”?

Closing: Secure Can Be Learned

The most hopeful message of the book is that attachment styles are stable but plastic. Understanding your style and your partner’s changes the question from “what’s wrong with me?” to “which need isn’t being met?”.

Start with one small step this week: state a single need honestly, clearly, and without blame. And remember this book is no substitute for therapy. If your old patterns feel too heavy, professional help is worth considering.
