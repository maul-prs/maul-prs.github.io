In Jerusalem, a settler locked in a bitter dispute was asked to play the part of her political opponent, and then listened to with real empathy for twenty minutes. Afterward she said she would consider giving up her land claims, if only her opponents could listen to her that way. No argument had softened her. Being understood had.

Introduction: A Language That Gives Life

Marshall Rosenberg, the psychologist behind Nonviolent Communication (NVC), began with a single question: what lets us stay compassionate even in the worst conditions, and what turns some people cruel? He found the answer in how we speak and how we listen. He went on to train teachers, doctors, parents, and communities in war zones, from Belgrade to Rwanda.

NVC is not a verbal technique. It is a way of steering attention to four things: what we observe, what we feel, what we need, and what we request. It works in two directions: expressing ourselves honestly, and hearing others with empathy.

Observation→Feeling→Need→Request
ObservationWhat happened, without judgment.
FeelingWhat I feel, not what I think.
NeedThe value beneath the feeling.
RequestA concrete action I ask for.
EmpathyHearing those four in someone else.
Self-compassionTurning all of it inward.
Takeaway: Before your next hard conversation, ask two things: what did I actually observe, and what do I actually need?

Part 1: Barriers to Compassion

Chapter 1: Giving from the Heart

At heart, we enjoy giving and receiving with compassion. NVC helps us return to that by directing awareness to the four components, so we speak and listen with respect and empathy. A teacher in Chicago faced with an aggressive student invited him to talk in “giraffe language,” and the boy calmly said, “Would you step back? I get angry when you stand that close.” The giraffe is NVC’s emblem because it has the biggest heart of any land animal.

Takeaway: Before you attack or go silent, ask what is alive in you and in the other person.

Chapter 2: Communication That Blocks Compassion

We are trained to speak in ways that push people apart. There are four forms: moralistic judgments (“she’s selfish”), comparisons, denial of responsibility (“I had no choice,” “it’s the rule”), and demands dressed up as requests. All of them pull us away from one thing: what is alive in us right now.

The heart of the barrier

A judgment is an expression of our own unmet needs, in a form that makes others defend themselves or hit back.

Takeaway: When you are about to call someone “lazy” or “selfish,” pause and ask: which of my needs is going unmet?

Part 2: Four Steps to Honest Speech

Chapter 3: Observing Without Evaluating

Observing without evaluating is the highest form of human intelligence.J. Krishnamurti, quoted by Rosenberg

Step one: separate observation from evaluation. Blend them and people hear criticism and fight back. Say “Hank hasn’t scored in twenty games,” not “Hank is a bad player.” When a workshop participant shouted that Rosenberg was the most arrogant speaker he had ever heard, Rosenberg didn’t defend himself. He guessed at the observation underneath: “Are you reacting because I talked for thirty minutes without giving you a turn?”

Takeaway: Swap “always,” “never,” and labels for a specific event a camera could record.

Chapter 4: Identifying and Expressing Feelings

Unspoken feelings come at a price, yet many of us lack the vocabulary. Compare “I’m sad you’re leaving,” which is a feeling, with “I feel you don’t love me,” which isn’t. It is a judgment about the other person in disguise. The words “I feel that…” usually introduce a thought, not a feeling.

Takeaway: Build a feeling vocabulary, and reach for words like sad, afraid, relieved, or disappointed rather than “neglected” or “used.”

Chapter 5: Taking Responsibility for Our Feelings

What others say or do may be the stimulus for our feelings, never the cause. The cause is our needs. When we hear a harsh message we have four options: blame ourselves, blame the other person, tune in to our own feelings and needs, or tune in to the feelings and needs behind the other person’s words. The last two open the way.

Emotional slavery→The obnoxious stage→Emotional liberation

Most of us pass through three stages: feeling responsible for others’ feelings, then turning defiant, and finally owning our own feelings fully without carrying everyone else’s.

Takeaway: Say “I feel … because I need …,” not “You make me …”.

Chapter 6: Requesting That Which Would Enrich Life

Step four: ask in clear, positive action language. Say what you want, not what you don’t. Check that your message was heard as meant, and offer it as a request, not a demand. The test is simple: someone who says no is not punished. NVC isn’t about getting people to comply. It is about building relationships on honesty and empathy.

Takeaway: End with a concrete request that can be done now, and accept a “no” without punishing.

Part 3: Receiving with Empathy

Chapter 7: Receiving Empathically

Don’t just do something, stand there.Buddhist saying, quoted by Rosenberg

Empathy is respectful understanding of what others are experiencing. Our habit is to advise, console, or tell our own story. Empathy asks us to empty the mind and listen with our whole being. Rosenberg learned this from his daughter, who looked in a mirror and said she was “as ugly as a pig.” Quick reassurance didn’t help. He learned to ask first whether advice was even wanted.

We cannot give empathy when we are running on empty. If listening is hard, stop and breathe, “scream” nonviolently, or take a break.

Takeaway: Listen for feelings and needs, not solutions. Reflect what you heard before offering any fix.

Chapter 8: The Power of Empathy

Empathy heals. It helps us stay vulnerable, defuses potential violence, hears “no” without taking it as rejection, revives a dead conversation, and even hears the message in silence. People often move past crippling pain when they meet someone who truly hears them.

Takeaway: Hear “no” as a need that is blocking “yes,” and ask what it is.

Part 4: Yourself, Anger, and Conflict

Chapter 9: Connecting Compassionately with Ourselves

Let us become the change we seek in the world.Mahatma Gandhi (chapter epigraph)

If we are harsh with ourselves, compassion for others is hard. Rosenberg tells of a new gray suit that ended up spotted by a pen he had left uncapped in his pocket. For twenty minutes he called himself careless and stupid. Then he asked what need lay beneath the scolding. It was to take better care of himself after rushing to serve others. The shame eased. That is NVC mourning: regretting without punishing yourself.

Another tool is turning “have to” into “choose to.” An act done from duty or guilt feels heavy. Name the need it serves, and it becomes meaningful play.

Takeaway: When you blame yourself, ask which need went unmet, and replace “I have to” with “I choose to … because ….”

Chapter 10: Expressing Anger Fully

Blaming and punishing are only shallow expressions of anger. The key: the other person is the stimulus of your anger, never the cause. The cause is your own judgment and your unmet need.

Stop and breathe→Spot the judgmental thoughts→Connect to the need→Express feelings and need
Extra step

Sometimes, before the last step, offer the other person empathy so they are ready to hear. And give yourself time to learn.

Takeaway: Don’t speak in the heat of anger. First name your judgment and the need behind it.

Chapter 11: Conflict Resolution and Mediation

Ordinary mediation gets lost in issues and compromises. NVC does it differently: first build a human connection, make sure both sides’ needs are voiced and heard, and only then look for strategies. If one party is too hurt to listen, offer empathy for as long as it takes. The final step is a specific, positive, doable request.

Takeaway: Separate needs from strategies. Hear every need fully before seeking solutions.

Chapter 12: The Protective Use of Force

When there is no chance to communicate, as in sudden danger, force may be used to protect. The intent is to prevent harm or injustice, not to punish so someone will repent or change. Two questions show the limits of punishment: “What do I want this person to do differently?” and “What do I want their reasons for doing it to be?” Punishment may answer the first but wrecks the second, because people then act to avoid the penalty.

Takeaway: Before punishing, ask what kind of reasons you hope will grow in that person.

Part 5: Freeing Ourselves and Celebrating

Chapter 13: Liberating Ourselves and Counseling Others

NVC turns negative internal messages into feelings and needs. Recognizing our own feelings and needs with empathy can free us from depression. For counselors and therapists, NVC replaces diagnosis with an honest, equal relationship.

Takeaway: When negative thoughts about yourself take over, translate them into: what am I feeling and needing right now?

Chapter 14: Expressing Appreciation

Ordinary praise is often a judgment, even when positive, and is sometimes used to manipulate. NVC appreciation is purely to celebrate. It has three parts: the action that enriched my life, the need it met, and the feeling of pleasure it brought. Receiving it, we celebrate too, without feeling superior or faking modesty. The book closes with Rosenberg’s uncle Julius, who learned compassion from his grandmother, who during the Depression took a tailor’s family into her home for three years.

Takeaway: Today, voice appreciation in three parts: what was done, which need it met, and what you feel.

Three Questions to Sit With

  1. Which judgment of others do I use most, and what need of mine is really underneath it?
  2. When did I last jump in with advice when someone only wanted to be heard?
  3. What am I doing out of “have to,” and what shifts if I change it to “choose to”?

Closing: Speak and Listen from the Heart

Nearly every wound in a relationship starts the same way: we were busy judging when we should have been understanding. NVC offers a simple road home. Observe without evaluating. Name the feeling. Find the need. Ask clearly. Then do the same when you listen to others, and when you listen to yourself.

You don’t have to be perfect. Start with one conversation this week, and remember the goal isn’t to win an argument but to build a connection where everyone’s needs have a place.

“…a very rare and difficult thing; it is almost a miracle.”Simone Weil, on giving attention to a sufferer, quoted by Rosenberg in Chapter 7
