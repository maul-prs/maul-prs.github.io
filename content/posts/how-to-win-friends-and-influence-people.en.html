“Two Gun” Crowley, a killer cornered by police, wrote a note before his capture saying that beneath his coat was a weary heart, but a kind one. Al Capone saw himself as a public benefactor, misunderstood. Even the most brutal criminals seldom blame themselves. So what is the use of criticizing anyone else?

Introduction: A Book About Handling People

Dale Carnegie taught public speaking to adults in New York, and he soon noticed his students needed something else even more: how to get along with people and win them over. His book came out in 1936 and is still read today, because it isn’t about tricks. It is about treating people as people. It has four parts and thirty principles in all.

Part 1 (3 principles)Fundamental techniques in handling people.
Part 2 (6 principles)Ways to make people like you.
Part 3 (12 principles)How to win people to your way of thinking.
Part 4 (9 principles)How to lead without giving offense.
Takeaway: Every principle in the book rests on one thing: pay attention to what matters to the other person, not only to you.

Part 1: Fundamental Techniques in Handling People

Principle 1: Don’t Criticize, Condemn, or Complain

Criticism puts people on the defensive, because it wounds their pride. Roosevelt blamed Taft, Taft felt blameless, and the quarrel split their party. Criticism is like a homing pigeon: it always comes home to us. If hard cases like Crowley and Capone never blamed themselves, what should we expect from ordinary people?

Takeaway: Before you criticize, remember that the person almost surely feels in the right. Try to understand rather than to judge.

Principle 2: Give Honest, Sincere Appreciation

Freud held that our actions spring from the sex urge and the desire to be great. John Dewey named the deepest human urge as the desire to be important. Charles Schwab was paid more than three thousand dollars a day for his gift of firing people up: he was lavish in approval and hearty in praise. Don’t confuse this with flattery, which is shallow, insincere, and selfish.

Takeaway: Today, find one thing genuinely worth appreciating in someone near you, and say it sincerely.

Principle 3: Arouse in the Other Person an Eager Want

…see things from that person’s angle as well as from your own.Henry Ford, quoted by Carnegie

The only way to influence people is to talk about what they want and show them how to get it. We fish with worms because fish like worms, not because we like strawberries.

Takeaway: Before you ask for something, ask yourself: what does this person want, and how does my request help them get it?

Principles 1–2: Be Interested, and Smile

…more friends in two months by becoming interested in other people…Dale Carnegie

A dog is the best friend-maker around: it sells nothing, it is simply glad to see us. Carnegie recalls Tippy, the little dog of his boyhood. The second principle is plain: smile. William Steinhardt, a stockbroker, had hardly smiled at his wife in all his years of marriage. After a week of trying, he found his home life happier. The smile has to be real, because a fake one fools no one.

Takeaway: Open conversations with real curiosity about the person, and don’t be stingy with your smile.

Principles 3–4: Names and Listening

Jim Farley lost his father young and had to work, yet he learned to remember thousands of names. That skill helped him in winning the White House for Roosevelt. His method: hear the name clearly, repeat it, write it down, and link it to the face. Then be a good listener. A person who only talks about himself is tiresome. A person who lets others tell their story is thought a brilliant conversationalist.

Takeaway: Use the person’s name in conversation, and ask questions that get them talking.

Principles 5–6: Their Interests and Their Importance

Theodore Roosevelt read up the night before on whatever his visitor cared about, so the guest felt understood. And then there is the golden rule: make the other person feel important, and do it sincerely. Nearly everyone craves recognition, so they remember whoever gives it.

Takeaway: Find out what interests the person, talk about it, and acknowledge what makes them feel significant.

Part 3: How to Win People to Your Way of Thinking

Principles 1–3: Avoid Arguments, Admit Mistakes

At a London dinner, Carnegie insisted on correcting a man who credited a Shakespeare line to the Bible. His friend, a Shakespeare expert, kicked him under the table and said, “Dale, you were wrong.” The lesson: the only way to win an argument is to avoid it. Even if you win, you lose, because the other person feels small and resents you. Show respect for other people’s opinions, never say “you’re wrong,” and if you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically.

The story of Rex

Carnegie often walked his dog Rex off the leash in the park. When a policeman rebuked him, he owned up at once before another word was said. The officer softened and let it go. Criticizing yourself first takes the wind out of an opponent.

Takeaway: When “you’re wrong” is on the tip of your tongue, hold it. And if you’re the one who is wrong, say so before anyone else does.

Principles 4–7: Be Friendly, Get “Yes,” Let Them Talk, Let It Be Their Idea

A drop of honey catches more flies than a gallon of gall.Abraham Lincoln, quoted by Carnegie

Begin in a friendly way. Woodrow Wilson put it roughly like this: come at me with clenched fists and mine will clench as fast as yours, but come and say, let’s sit down and talk it over, and we soon find our differences are not so great. Then use the Socratic method: ask questions that draw a “yes” until the other person reaches the conclusion on his own. James Eberson, a bank teller, opened an account for a customer who had refused to give any information, using exactly this approach.

Let the other person do much of the talking, since he knows more about his own problem. And let him feel the idea is his. Colonel House, Wilson’s adviser, planted ideas and let Wilson claim them, which kept his influence intact.

Takeaway: Plant ideas through questions, let people discover them, and don’t fight over credit.

Principles 8–10: Empathy and Nobler Motives

Try honestly to see things from the other person’s point of view, and be sympathetic with his ideas and desires. The magic formula: “I don’t blame you one iota for feeling as you do. If I were you I would feel just as you do.” It calms nearly everyone. Then appeal to nobler motives. People rarely admit to unworthy reasons, so we help them become better by trusting their good intentions.

Takeaway: Start with “I can see why you feel that way,” then make your request.

Principles 11–12: Dramatize and Throw Down a Challenge

Facts alone aren’t enough. Dramatize your ideas with pictures, demonstrations, or vivid stories. And when everything else fails, throw down a challenge. High achievers love competition and a chance to prove themselves.

Takeaway: Turn your numbers and appeals into something people can see, and give them a chance to prove themselves.

Part 4: Be a Leader Without Giving Offense

Principles 1–3: Praise First, Correct Indirectly, Own Your Mistakes

President Coolidge once praised his secretary’s dress, then added, “But I’m sure you can be just as careful with your punctuation.” Praise first makes the criticism go down easier, like the dentist’s novocaine before the drill. Call attention to mistakes indirectly, and talk about your own mistakes before criticizing. Hearing that the boss has erred too makes people readier to fix theirs.

Takeaway: Build the correction in order: honest praise, indirect criticism, then admit that you have erred too.

Principles 4–5: Ask Questions and Let People Save Face

Swap orders for questions: “Do you think this would work?” People who help shape a decision are keener to carry it out. And never shame anyone. Let the other person save face. Wounding someone’s dignity leaves a long-lasting hurt, even if we are right.

Takeaway: Correct in private, not before others, and leave a dignified way out.

Principles 6–9: Praise Progress, Give a Fine Reputation, Encourage

Praise the slightest improvement, and give people a fine reputation to live up to. People tend to match the image we hand them. Encourage them, make faults seem easy to correct, and make what you ask for something they will be glad to do.

Why this works

All the leadership principles here run on a single mechanism: people grow when they feel valued and trusted, not when they feel judged.

Takeaway: Pick one person, praise a small step of progress today, and name the good reputation you hope they will keep.

Three Questions to Sit With

  1. When did I last criticize someone, and what happened to our relationship afterward?
  2. Who deserves sincere appreciation from me today, and what will I say?
  3. In my last conversation, how long did I talk compared to how long I listened?

Closing: Start with One Person

Thirty principles sound like a lot, but they all circle one idea: people want to feel important and understood. Give them that sincerely, and the relationships around you begin to change.

Carnegie himself urged readers not to read the book once and put it away. Take one principle a week, practice it, and watch what happens. You don’t have to be perfect. Just start with one person today.
